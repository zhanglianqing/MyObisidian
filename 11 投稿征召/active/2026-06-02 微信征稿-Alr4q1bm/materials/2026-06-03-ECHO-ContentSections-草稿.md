## Content Sections · 粘贴正文（约 527/750 词）

### Introduction

```
The hippocampus plays a central role in memory and emotion regulation, yet hippocampal dysfunction is well documented in major depressive disorder (MDD), including reduced volume and blunted activation during positive autobiographical memory (AM) recall. Because of its deep location, the hippocampus is difficult to target with conventional non-invasive neuromodulation. Real-time fMRI neurofeedback (rt-fMRI-NF) offers a non-invasive endogenous approach to engage hippocampal function. However, optimal protocols for hippocampal regulation and their effects on mood remain underexplored. This preliminary study examined the feasibility, behavioral effects, and neural mechanisms of a hippocampal rt-fMRI-NF protocol using positive AM recall in healthy young adults.
```

### Methods

```
Nineteen healthy right-handed college students (13 female; mean age = 21.47 ± 1.12 years) without Axis I disorders completed a two-day protocol approved by the Sichuan University ethics committee. On Day 1, participants selected positive AMs and completed one practice run. On Day 2, they completed four block-design training runs alternating Up-Regulation (40 s), Serial Subtraction (SS; 40 s), and Rest (40 s). During Up-Regulation, participants recalled positive AMs to increase left hippocampal percent signal change (PSC) relative to the immediately preceding Rest block, following standard block-design rt-fMRI-NF practice. Run 3 was a transfer run without feedback. Data were acquired on a 3 T United Imaging scanner using OpenNFT for online feedback. Pre- and post-training Self-Rating Depression and Anxiety Scales (SDS/SAS) and the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) were compared. Offline analyses used SPM12 ROI and whole-brain GLM; the primary offline contrast was Up-Regulation > SS, because an active cognitive baseline provides a more stable hippocampal contrast than wakeful rest.
```

### Results

```
Single-session training was associated with significant reductions in depression (mean reduction = 4.58, p = 0.003) and anxiety (mean reduction = 4.11, p < 0.001). Greater improvement was predicted by higher baseline distress (ΔSDS, r = 0.54, p = 0.020; ΔSAS, r = 0.64, p = 0.004) and lower expressive suppression (ΔSAS, r = -0.705, p = 0.001). Serial Subtraction consistently deactivated the left hippocampus (all p < 0.05), yielding significant Up-Regulation > SS engagement in early runs (practice: p = 0.007; Run 1: p = 0.016). Hippocampal engagement against this active baseline correlated with depression improvement (final-run beta vs ΔSDS, r = 0.57, p = 0.022; mean feedback-period activity vs ΔSDS, r = 0.56, p = 0.026). Up-Regulation > Rest was not significant in the target hippocampus (all p > 0.21), supporting use of an active baseline. Whole-brain analyses showed engagement of cognitive control regions (SMA, insula, cerebellum) and deactivation of default mode network nodes, with practice-related adaptation in anterior cingulate and posterior cingulate/precuneus.
```

### Discussion/Conclusion

```
Brief hippocampal rt-fMRI-NF training improved self-reported mood in healthy young adults, especially among participants with higher baseline distress. Under an active-control design, hippocampal engagement during positive AM recall relative to serial subtraction was the primary neural finding and was associated with behavioral improvement, whereas Up-Regulation > Rest was not significant in the target ROI. Real-time feedback was computed relative to preceding Rest blocks per conventional practice; offline inference therefore emphasized Up-Regulation > SS. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of hippocampal-targeted endogenous neuromodulation and underscore the importance of baseline choice when regulating DMN-related deep targets. Future randomized controlled trials in clinical populations are warranted.
```
